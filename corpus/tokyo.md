# Tokyo

## Understand

Tokyo is Japan's capital and the world's largest urban area, with roughly 37 million people in the Greater Tokyo region. The city blends centuries-old temples with glass towers, and formal business etiquette with the subcultures of Harajuku and Akihabara. Called Edo until 1868, Tokyo was flattened twice in the 20th century — by the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake and by WWII firebombing — then rebuilt rapidly. That history explains why most buildings are modern despite the city's long past, and why green spaces are precious and dispersed. The city has an oceanic climate with four distinct seasons.

## Best Time to Visit

- **Spring (late March to April):** Cherry blossom season (*sakura*). Beautiful but crowded; accommodation and flights spike. Book 3–6 months ahead.
- **Autumn (October to November):** Cool, clear, dry. Often considered the best season — foliage peaks mid-November. Fewer crowds than spring.
- **Summer (June to August):** Hot and humid (highs 30–35°C / 86–95°F). Short rainy season in June (*tsuyu*). Summer festivals are a draw; typhoons possible August–September.
- **Winter (December to February):** Cold but dry (lows around 0°C / 32°F). Minimal snow in central Tokyo. Cheapest for flights outside the New Year's week.
- **Avoid:** Golden Week (late April to early May), Obon (mid-August), and New Year (Dec 29–Jan 3) — massive domestic travel surges, high prices, many businesses closed.

## Neighborhoods

Tokyo is best understood as a collection of neighborhoods, each with distinct character, rather than a city with one center. The Yamanote Line (JR's circular train route) links the main ones.

- **Shinjuku:** Western hub. Government buildings, the world's busiest train station, Kabukichō nightlife.
- **Shibuya:** Youth and fashion. The scramble crossing, Harajuku next door, Omotesandō for luxury brands.
- **Ginza:** Upscale shopping, historic department stores, Michelin-starred dining. Pedestrianized on weekends.
- **Asakusa:** Old-city feel. Sensō-ji temple, traditional crafts.
- **Akihabara:** Electronics and otaku (anime, manga, gaming) culture.
- **Roppongi:** Nightlife, international business, art museums.
- **Shimokitazawa and Kōenji:** Western subculture — thrift shops, indie music, cafes. Where locals go.
- **Yanaka and Nezu:** Pockets of pre-war Tokyo. Narrow lanes, small temples, slow pace.

## Get in

Two airports serve Tokyo:

- **Narita (NRT):** Most international flights, 60–90 minutes from central Tokyo. Options: Narita Express (JR, ~60 min, included in JR Pass), Keisei Skyliner (fastest at ~40 min to Ueno), limousine buses, or taxi (very expensive).
- **Haneda (HND):** Closer (~30 min to central Tokyo) and increasingly used for international flights. Monorail to Hamamatsuchō, Keikyu Line to Shinagawa. Prefer Haneda if you can choose.

## Get around

Tokyo's rail network is dense, punctual, and clean. You will not need taxis for most trips.

- **Trains and metro:** Multiple operators (JR, Tokyo Metro, Toei Subway) interoperate through IC cards (Suica or Pasmo). Tap on and off — they also work on buses and vending machines.
- **Rush hour:** 7:30–9:30 AM and 5:30–7:30 PM are legendarily packed.
- **Lines you'll use most:** Yamanote (circular loop), Chūō (east-west), Ginza Line (Asakusa to Shibuya).
- **Taxis:** Clean and metered. Rear doors open automatically — don't touch them. English is rare.
- **Walking:** Rewards neighborhood-level exploration.
- **Avoid driving.** Parking is expensive, addresses unintuitive.

## See

Highlights that most trips include (not exhaustive):

- Sensō-ji temple in Asakusa — Tokyo's oldest, with Nakamise shopping street leading up to it
- Meiji Shrine beside Harajuku — forested Shinto shrine honoring the Meiji emperor
- Shibuya scramble crossing — best viewed from the overlooking Starbucks or Shibuya Sky
- Tokyo Skytree — tallest tower in Japan, observation decks
- Tsukiji Outer Market — food stalls and tool shops; the wholesale market moved to Toyosu
- Imperial Palace East Gardens — free to enter, remnants of Edo Castle
- Shinjuku Gyoen — large park, especially good in cherry blossom and autumn

## Do

Experiences and concrete day activities:

- **Visit an onsen or sentō** (hot spring or public bath). Wash thoroughly before entering, no swimwear. Tattoos may be refused — some places are tattoo-friendly, check ahead.
- **Spend an evening at an izakaya.** Order small plates — sashimi, grilled skewers, karaage — with beer or sake. A small seating charge (*otōshi*, ~¥300–500) is normal and comes with an appetizer.
- **TeamLab Planets or TeamLab Borderless.** Immersive digital art museums. Book online in advance.
- **Yanaka Ginza shopping street.** Afternoon stroll through old-Tokyo alleys with small shops and food stalls.
- **Shimokitazawa browse.** Spend a half day in thrift shops, used book stores, and indie cafes.
- **Rent a kimono in Asakusa.** Rental shops near Sensō-ji include dressing and photos.
- **Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building observation decks.** Free, 45th floor, panoramic city views.
- **Attend a sumo match.** Ryōgoku Kokugikan hosts three Tokyo tournaments a year (January, May, September).
- **Studio Ghibli Museum.** Book months ahead through the official lottery — tickets sell out fast.
- **Karaoke box.** Private rooms with friends; order drinks and food from a tablet.
- **Wander Golden Gai or Omoide Yokochō.** Tiny alleyways of miniature bars seating six to eight people each. Some have cover charges and no-tourist policies.

## Day Trips

Trips doable in a day from central Tokyo:

- **Kamakura (~1 hr):** Giant Buddha (outdoors since 1495), coastal temples. Enoshima island nearby.
- **Nikkō (~2 hrs):** Mountain town with the elaborate Tōshō-gū shrine complex (UNESCO), hot springs, Kegon Falls.
- **Hakone (~1.5 hrs):** Hot-spring resort area, Open-Air Museum, Lake Ashi with Mt Fuji views on clear days.
- **Mt Fuji / Kawaguchiko (~2 hrs):** Lake with the classic Mt Fuji reflection view. Climbing Fuji is July to early September only.
- **Yokohama (~30 min):** Japan's largest Chinatown, waterfront, Cup Noodles Museum.
- **Enoshima (~1 hr):** Small island with caves, shrines, beach-town atmosphere.

## Eat & Drink

Tokyo has more Michelin stars than any city in the world, but the breadth of everyday food culture matters more.

**Dining basics:**

- **Breakfast is minimal.** Convenience store onigiri, a coffee, or a quick cafe sandwich is standard.
- **Lunch sets (*teishoku*):** Fixed-price lunch sets at ¥800–1,500 are the office-worker standard. Picture menus or plastic food displays are common.
- **Dinner is longer.** Izakaya, sushi counters, or ramen shops. Ramen ¥800–1,500; sushi ranges from conveyor belt (¥100–500/plate) to omakase (¥10,000+).

**Dishes to try:** sushi, ramen, tempura, tonkatsu, soba, udon, yakitori, okonomiyaki, monjayaki (Tokyo specialty), unagi, and kaiseki.

**Etiquette:** Slurping ramen is encouraged. Don't stick chopsticks upright in rice (funeral imagery). Don't tip — it can offend. Say *itadakimasu* before eating, *gochisōsama deshita* after.

**Konbini:** 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson. Food is high quality, fresh, and cheap — reliable backup for any meal.

**Drinks:**

- **Beer:** Asahi, Kirin, Sapporo dominate. Draft (*nama biiru*) is standard at izakaya.
- **Sake:** Served hot (*atsukan*) or cold (*reishu*). Order by region or brewery, not just "sake."
- **Shōchū:** Distilled spirit, often from sweet potato or barley. Mixed with hot water or soda.
- **Highballs:** Whisky and soda — hugely popular and cheap, the standard "quick drink."
- **Coffee:** Strong cafe culture. Blue Bottle and % Arabica are among the local chains alongside Starbucks. Kissaten (traditional coffee shops) are slower, formal, no laptops.
- **Nomihōdai:** All-you-can-drink deals (typically two hours) are common at izakaya chains, usually ¥1,500–2,500.

## Budget and Costs

Approximate daily spend per person:

- **Budget:** ¥7,500–12,000/day. Hostel or capsule hotel, konbini meals, ramen for dinner, trains only.
- **Mid-range:** ¥18,000–30,000/day. Business hotel or Airbnb, mix of casual and sit-down meals, museum entries, occasional taxi.
- **Luxury:** ¥60,000+/day. 4- to 5-star hotel, kaiseki or omakase dinners, private tours, taxis.

**Common costs:**

- Metro single ride: ¥170–320
- Tokyo Metro day pass: ¥800
- Ramen bowl: ¥800–1,500
- Lunch set: ¥1,000–2,000
- Budget hotel: ¥8,000–12,000/night
- Mid-range hotel: ¥20,000–35,000/night
- Beer at izakaya: ¥500–800
- Convenience store meal: ¥500–800
- Museum entry: ¥500–2,000

## Practical Travel Information

- **Currency:** Japanese yen (¥). Cash is still widely preferred at smaller restaurants, temples, and markets, though cards and IC cards work in major shops.
- **ATMs:** 7-Eleven and Japan Post Bank ATMs accept foreign cards. Many bank ATMs do not.
- **Tipping:** Not practiced and can offend. Service is included; don't leave cash on the table.
- **Dress code:** Casual is fine most places, but Japanese dress neatly even casually. Temples and shrines: modest, shoulders covered. Nicer restaurants (kaiseki, omakase): smart casual. Shoes are removed when entering homes, temples, and some restaurants — slip-ons save hassle.
- **Electrical:** Type A/B plugs (same as US). 100V, 50Hz east / 60Hz west. US devices work; UK/EU need an adapter.
- **Internet:** Pocket wifi rental at airports (~¥500/day) or prepaid SIM. Konbini and major stations have free wifi.
- **Toilets:** High-tech bidet toilets are common. Occasional squat toilets in older stations and temples.
- **Garbage:** Few public bins. Carry your trash until a konbini (which has separation bins) or back to your hotel.
- **Smoking:** Banned in most restaurants and public streets; designated smoking areas are marked.

## Stay safe

Tokyo is one of the safest major cities in the world. Violent crime is rare.

- **Pickpocketing:** Uncommon but not zero in dense tourist areas.
- **Earthquakes:** Small tremors are routine. Larger ones are rare but possible. Drop, cover, hold on; buildings are built to strict code. The Japan Meteorological Agency sends phone alerts.
- **Typhoons:** June through October, with the peak in September. Trains and flights are cancelled; stay indoors.
- **Scams:** Kabukichō (Shinjuku) has aggressive bar touts — don't follow strangers to clubs offering drinks.
- **Lost items:** Report at the nearest police box (*kōban*). Japan has one of the highest lost-property return rates in the world.

## Talk

- **Language:** Japanese. Writing uses kanji, hiragana, and katakana.
- **English prevalence:** Lower than you would expect for a global city. Station signs are in English; menus in major areas often are; conversational English is spotty outside hotels. Younger people and tourism staff manage better.
- **Useful phrases:** *sumimasen* (excuse me / sorry), *arigatō gozaimasu* (thank you), *onegaishimasu* (please), *eigo o hanasemasu ka* (do you speak English?).
- **Apps:** Google Translate's camera feature works well on menus.
- **Courtesy:** A slight bow when thanking or apologizing. Business cards (*meishi*) are handed and received with two hands. Keep voice low on trains.
